Ischaemic stroke: support in Queensland

The most common type of stroke, caused by a blockage in an artery supplying the brain. In-home and community support for plan-managed and self-managed NDIS participants.

Call 0450 521 274
Understanding ischaemic stroke

What it is, and what it means for recovery.

An ischaemic stroke happens when a clot or narrowing cuts off blood flow to part of the brain. Brain cells in that area begin to be damaged within minutes, which is why fast treatment matters. Around 8 in 10 strokes are ischaemic.

Clots can form in the brain's own arteries, or travel from the heart or neck arteries. Common contributing factors include high blood pressure, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, high cholesterol and smoking. Some strokes have no clear cause.

The effects depend on which artery is blocked and how quickly blood flow returns. Some people recover a great deal in the first months. Others live with lasting changes to movement, speech, vision, thinking or energy.

Think someone is having a stroke? Call 000 immediately. Use FAST: Face, Arms, Speech, Time.
Daily life

How it can affect daily life.

Movement and strength

Weakness or reduced control on one side of the body is common, affecting walking, dressing and using an arm or hand.

Speech and understanding

Some people have difficulty finding words or understanding speech, or their speech is slurred.

Energy

Fatigue after stroke is often more than tiredness, and it can limit what a person manages in a day.

Thinking and mood

Memory, attention and planning can change, and low mood and anxiety are common.

Risk of another stroke

Managing blood pressure, medicines and appointments matters, and routines often need to support that.

How we support

How we support people living with this.

Daily living support

Personal care, dressing and routines, done the way the person prefers and using the techniques their therapists teach.

Therapy carry-over

Practising exercises set by the OT, physiotherapist or speech pathologist through the week, so progress does not stall between sessions.

Hospital and rehab to home

Meeting the person and the ward team before discharge, and having a familiar worker in place on day one.

Appointments and transport

Getting to rehabilitation, specialist and GP appointments, and following through on what was agreed.

Carer breaks

Dependable support at home so family carers can rest, work or look after their own health.

Our team

Our team is trained for stroke.

  • Every worker completes a stroke-specific induction before their first shift.
  • Workers learn the person's therapy plan, swallowing plan and communication approach first.
  • Cade McMillan reviews every referral personally and is the named contact.
  • We work with the OT, physiotherapist and speech pathologist, and we do not replace them.
Funding

NDIS support and stroke.

Who can apply to the NDIS

  • People aged under 65 who live in Australia and are Australian citizens, permanent residents or Protected Special Category Visa holders.
  • Those with a permanent impairment that significantly affects daily life. Evidence from the treating team is important.
  • People who are already NDIS participants can stay after 65 if they entered the scheme before then.

Questions

Questions about ischaemic stroke

Is ischaemic stroke the same as a blocked artery?

Yes, in simple terms. A blockage or severe narrowing reduces blood flow to part of the brain. Doctors will work out the cause, because that guides prevention.

Can people recover after an ischaemic stroke?

Many people improve, most noticeably in the first months, and gains can continue for longer with regular practice. How much varies a lot from person to person, so we follow the treating team's advice rather than predicting outcomes.

What support might someone need at home?

It varies. Some people need help with personal care and meals. Others mainly need encouragement to practise therapy exercises, transport to appointments and company in the community.

Can I use NDIS funding for support after an ischaemic stroke?

If the person is an NDIS participant with funding for daily living or community supports, and their plan is plan-managed or self-managed, yes.
